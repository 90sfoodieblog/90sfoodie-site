Cherry Coke

The flavor never left. The loudest can in America did.

Still sold!
MakerCoca-Cola
Years1985–today · the 90s can was the icon
StatusStill sold
ShelfDrinks

Cherry Coke never got discontinued. You can buy it today, in a clean, modern, well-behaved can, and it tastes like Cherry Coke. So why does showing a 90s kid the old can — that bold swirl of purple and pink with the lightning streaking across it — produce the same feeling as showing them a photograph of a childhood home long gone?

Read the original post on Substack

The story

Because the flavor survived and the can did not, and the can was half the experience. The Cherry Coke of the 1990s did not sit quietly on the shelf between the colas. It shouted. It was magenta. It had weather on it. It looked less like a beverage than like the poster for a summer action movie, and every kid who drank from it understood instinctively that they were holding the wildest object the soda aisle was legally allowed to sell.

We keep a whole shelf at www.90sfoodie.com for drinks that are still with us but no longer themselves, and Cherry Coke is the patron saint of the category: a survivor wearing a ghost's wardrobe.

The Upstart That Earned Its Seat

Start with the genuinely impressive part: Cherry Coke was not supposed to work at all. When Coca-Cola launched it in 1985, it was the company's first major flavored extension of the flagship — a line the soda world treated as sacred — and it arrived in the same turbulent season as the New Coke debacle. In that chaos, Cherry Coke quietly did the opposite of failing. It became a phenomenon, especially with younger drinkers, and it never really left. Before Cherry Coke, "cola, but cherry" was a soda-fountain improvisation, the thing you asked for at diners where the syrup lived behind the counter. After Cherry Coke, it was a permanent category, and every rival had to answer it.

That fountain history is the flavor's soul. Cherry cola tastes like a customization — like somebody in a paper hat made your drink *yours*. The can industrialized a small act of rebellion, and the 90s design took that rebellion and gave it a uniform. Which brings us to the can itself, because the can is why we are really here.

Anatomy of a Loud Can

Set the 90s Cherry Coke can beside any soda can sold today and the modern one looks like it is apologizing. The 90s design ran a diagonal "Cherry Coke" script in enormous white letters across a storm of purple, magenta, and hot pink, with jagged yellow lightning bolts striking through the background like the soda had been photographed during a thunderstorm at a rave. It was not elegant. It was not supposed to be. It was a can designed by people who understood that their customer was fourteen, considered subtlety a form of surrender, and chose drinks the way they chose sneakers.

That design belonged to a whole era of packaging maximalism that the archive at www.90sfoodie.com documents with real affection — the years when soft drinks dressed like extreme sports. Sodas had lightning, splashes, chrome, and attitude. The can was a collectible object before anyone used that word for groceries; kids kept these cans, lined them up, crushed them with ceremony. The drink inside was consistent and dependable. The can was the concert shirt.

And here is the quiet tragedy of beverage branding: flavors are forever, but designs are temporary. Cherry Coke's recipe earned a lifetime appointment. Its wardrobe got rotated out like every generation of packaging, each redesign calmer than the last, until the modern can arrived — tasteful, minimal, and completely incapable of starting a conversation in a lunchroom.

The Flavor That Outlived Its Own Poster

There is a deeper reason the old can hits so hard, and it is about what Cherry Coke represented in the soda hierarchy of the 90s. Regular Coke belonged to everybody — parents, restaurants, the world. Cherry Coke belonged to *us*. It was the first cola that felt youth-specific without being a kids' drink, sweet but with an edge, mainstream but with a streak of diner-counter mischief in it. The purple can made that allegiance visible from across the cafeteria. You were not just drinking a soda; you were flying a flag, and the flag was magenta.

Brands chase that feeling now with limited editions and artist collaborations, but the 90s version was more honest: it was the everyday can, all year, at regular price. The wildness was not a promotion. It was the product's permanent personality — until, one redesign at a time, it wasn't. Readers write to www.90sfoodie.com about Cherry Coke more than almost any still-living drink, and the letters are never really about cherry. They are about the discovery that something can survive and still be gone.

That paradox is the engine of this whole archive. Discontinuation is obvious. Redesign is sneakier: the name stays, the flavor stays, the UPC stays — and the object you loved is quietly replaced by its own descendant. If Ecto Cooler is the king of drinks that vanished, Cherry Coke is the king of drinks that stayed and changed, which some days feels like the greater loss. At least a discontinued drink admits it left.

Bring Back the Can, Keep the Soda

Here is the modest proposal we float in the comments at www.90sfoodie.com whenever the magenta can makes the rounds: Coca-Cola does not need to change a drop of the liquid. Just press the 90s design back into service — the storm, the lightning, the diagonal script, all of it — as the can's permanent retro edition, and watch what happens. The company already knows this works; throwback packaging is the one nostalgia lever the soda giants pull with total confidence, because it asks nothing of the recipe and everything of the memory. Surge came back on fan demand alone. Crystal Pepsi got its encore. A *can design* is the easiest comeback in the business: no formula to protect, no supply chain to rebuild, just ink.

Until then, the flavor endures and the storm stays in photographs. Cherry Coke is still a great soda. It is simply wearing a suit now, and some of us remember when it wore lightning.

So tell us, in the comments: does Cherry Coke still count as Cherry Coke to you in the modern can — or did the drink you loved technically end the day the purple storm retired? And which current soda can is the *least* worthy successor to that era's throne?
