Fruit String Thing

The snack you had to make before you could eat.

Gone, not forgotten
MakerBetty Crocker
Years1990s–2000s
StatusDiscontinued
ShelfChips & Snacks

Fruit String Thing came with one instruction that never needed printing on the box: do not eat this yet. First, you had to make something out of it.

Read the original post on Substack

The story

In a decade of snacks engineered to be played with, Betty Crocker's strangest little invention might have been the purest. It was not shaped like a character. It did not squirt, foam, fizz, or change color. It was a string — one long, red, chewy coil of fruit snack wound up on its tray like a garden hose — and its entire marketing plan was the confidence that a kid, given a string, will invent the rest. Peel it. Stretch it. Spell your name with it. That was the product, and for a while in the 90s, it was lunchroom gold.

We keep a shelf in the archive at www.90sfoodie.com for the snacks that were secretly toys, and String Thing might be the one that understood the assignment best: it never told you what to make. It just handed you the material.

A Snack Sold as a String

The mechanics deserve respect, because they were so simple they were easy to miss. Inside the box, the fruit snack came as one continuous strand, coiled flat. You peeled it up slowly — the peel was the first pleasure, and rushing it was the first tragedy, because a snapped string was a shortened canvas. Once free, the strand was yours: a soft, pliable, strawberry-red rope with just enough stretch to hold a shape and just enough chew to remind you it was, technically, food.

Kids made the predictable things first. Bracelets. Necklaces. Eyeglasses. Letters, then whole names, laid out on the lunch table like edible handwriting. Then the art got ambitious — lassos for action figures, jump ropes for smaller toys, elaborate knots that took the whole lunch period and were eaten in ninety seconds. Eating it was almost the epilogue. The snack was the sculpting; the chewing was just how the sculpture got home.

There had been edible playthings before, but most of them told you what they were: a candy necklace is a necklace, a ring pop is a ring. String Thing arrived with no instructions and no mascot-shaped mold to copy. It was the rare snack that treated the kid as the designer, and that trust is exactly why people who write to www.90sfoodie.com about it do not describe a flavor first. They describe a creation.

The Lunchroom Test

Every 90s snack had to survive the lunchroom economy, and String Thing was unusually strong currency because it was a group activity disguised as an individual snack. One coil could entertain a whole table. Friends commissioned letters. Trades were negotiated in inches — a length of string for a length of string, flavor for flavor, with strawberry, the flagship on the box, holding the steadiest exchange rate.

It also passed the test that mattered most to the adults buying it. The box said Betty Crocker, the most trusted name in the American kitchen, and it said "made with fruit" in plain letters, which in the 90s was enough to move a product from the candy category into the lunchbox category in a parent's mind. Parents thought they were packing a fruit snack. Kids knew they were packing a craft supply that happened to be delicious. Both were right, and that double identity is why the box moved.

Compare its shelf-mates and the genius sharpens. The great Betty Crocker and General Mills fruit snacks of the era each came with a verb. Fruit Roll-Ups were for peeling. Fruit by the Foot was for measuring — three feet of fun, unrolled like a tape. Gushers were for biting into and bursting. String Thing's verb was the best of the set: making. The others you consumed. This one you authored, then consumed your own work, which is a far more memorable thing for a ten-year-old to do at noon.

Gone Without a Goodbye

String Thing's exit followed the standard discontinued-snack script, which is to say there was no script at all. No farewell flavor, no announcement — just thinner shelf presence sometime in the 2000s, then absence, then the slow realization among people restocking their childhoods that the coil was not coming back on the next grocery trip either. The archive page taking shape at www.90sfoodie.com hears the same story about it constantly: nobody remembers it leaving. Everybody remembers noticing it was gone.

The reasons, as always, were probably unglamorous. Fruit snacks consolidated into pouches and bites — formats that are cheaper to make, easier to portion, and far less likely to be worn as jewelry. A snack whose value depends on being one long unbroken strand is a manufacturing diva in a category that rewards uniformity. The string was the point, and the string was the problem.

But notice what the category lost with it. Bites and pouches are snacks you eat on the way to something else. String Thing demanded a table, a pause, and an idea. It was slow food for the fastest eaters alive, and nothing in the modern fruit snack aisle has replaced its particular verb. You can still buy a hundred fruit snacks. You cannot buy one that asks you to make something first.

The String Has No Heir

This is the part that surprises people: in an era when every discontinued snack seems to get a revival, a petition, or at least a limited online run, String Thing mostly gets longing. Maybe the format is genuinely hard to revive cheaply. Maybe the rights and the machinery scattered. Or maybe the snack industry looked at a product whose entire purpose was to delay eating and concluded, correctly, that it belongs to a slower childhood — the same childhood the rest of the archive is busy documenting before the witnesses forget the details.

We think it deserves better than a footnote, because String Thing proved something the whole decade kept proving in different aisles: kids did not want food that merely tasted good. They wanted food with a first act. The string was all first act.

So here is our standing question, the one that runs whenever String Thing comes up in the comments at www.90sfoodie.com: what did you make with yours? A name, a necklace, a work of lunchroom art too good to eat and eaten anyway? And if Betty Crocker brought the coil back tomorrow, one long strand, no instructions — are you still a maker, or has adulthood converted you to eating it straight off the tray like a civilian?
