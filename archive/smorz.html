Smorz

S'mores in a cereal bowl — no campfire required.

Gone, not forgotten
MakerKellogg's
Years2003–2013
StatusDiscontinued
ShelfCereals

The box says it in the biggest letters it can find: "YOU ASKED FOR IT — It's BACK!!" And that tells you almost everything about Smorz before you even pour a bowl. This is a cereal with a comeback story — actually, two of them. Kellogg's s'mores cereal has been discontinued, mourned, petitioned back, discontinued again, and petitioned back again, because its fans simply would not take no for an answer.

The story

Smorz is crunchy graham cereal squares wrapped in a rich chocolatey coating, tumbling with marshmallows — a campfire built for the breakfast table. It debuted in 2003, but its roots run straight through the 90s cereal aisle, and its afterlife is one of the great fan-driven comebacks in food history.

The idea of s'mores in a cereal bowl predates Smorz by decades. General Mills' S'mores Crunch launched back in 1982 — graham-flavored cereal pieces with marshmallows — and ran through 1988 before disappearing. It came back in various forms through the late 90s, then vanished again, leaving a s'mores-shaped hole in the cereal aisle that lasted for years.

That hole mattered. S'mores are one of America's foundational flavor memories — graham, chocolate, marshmallow, campfire — and every few years some cereal maker tries to bottle it. The attempts kept coming and going, but nothing quite stuck. By the early 2000s, the aisle was ready for someone to get it right.

Kellogg's launched Smorz in 2003, and the construction was the selling point. The box described it as "crunchy graham cereal wrapped in rich chocolatey coating with marshmallows," and that was exactly what you got: graham-flavored corn squares with a chocolate coating, mixed with marshmallows that started out plain white and later became brown-and-white swirled, like chocolate had been stirred through them.

The flavor worked because it understood the assignment. The graham brought the toasty, honeyed base. The chocolate coating brought the richness — sparing, not overwhelming, closer to a glaze than a shell. And the marshmallows brought the soft, sweet contrast that made every spoonful a little different. Eaten with milk, it was a genuine s'mores impression: not a candy bar in a bowl, but the actual campfire treat, translated.

At the height of production, the factory was going through thousands of pounds of chocolate cream and marshmallows every single day. Smorz had found its people.

Eaten with milk, Smorz was a study in contrast. The graham squares held their crunch well — the chocolate coating acted like a raincoat, keeping the milk at bay for the first few minutes — while the marshmallows went soft almost immediately, turning pillowy and sweet against the crisp squares. The milk at the bottom turned into a chocolate-graham situation that was arguably a fourth food group.

Eaten dry, straight from the box, it was a different animal: more like a snack mix, the chocolate coating more pronounced, the marshmallows chewy rather than soft. Both versions worked, which is rarer than it sounds — plenty of cereals are built for milk and fall apart without it. Smorz was a two-mode cereal, and kids of the 2000s ran it in both.

In December 2013, Kellogg's discontinued Smorz. The plan, such as it was, involved steering s'mores fans toward a new Krave S'mores cereal — a graham-flavored Krave with chocolate and marshmallow filling. But Krave S'mores was a different experience wearing a similar costume, and Smorz fans knew the difference immediately.

The mourning period lasted about two years. Then, in December 2015, Kellogg's announced Smorz was coming back — and the packaging said the quiet part loud: "You asked for it — It's BACK!!" The fans had won round one.

The victory was short-lived. In April 2019, Kellogg's discontinued Smorz a second time. If the first cancellation had a replacement strategy, the second one just felt like the end — another beloved cereal filed away in the archive of things they don't make anymore.

But Smorz fans had been through this before, and they knew the playbook. A Change.org petition gathered thousands of signatures. Social media filled with pleas. Kellogg's own marketing director would later admit the fanbase was "incredibly passionate" — corporate-speak for "they would not leave us alone."

In December 2020, Kellogg's caved again: Smorz would return for a third run, hitting shelves in January 2021. Same crunchy graham squares, same chocolatey coating, same marshmallows. The box once again carried the message the fans had earned.

Most discontinued cereals get one farewell. Smorz has had three launches because it nailed something specific: it is not just a flavor, it is a memory delivery system. S'mores belong to childhood summers — the fire, the sticky fingers, the charred marshmallow — and Smorz packed all of that into a 9.8-ounce box for $3.99.

There's also the underdog math. Every discontinuation made the fanbase louder and more organized. The 2013 cancellation taught them that petitions work. The 2019 cancellation proved they would do it again. Kellogg's now knows that ending Smorz comes with a cost measured in signatures and hashtags — which is, honestly, a healthier relationship between a company and its customers than most cereals ever get.

Pour a bowl today and the experience is the same one the box has been promising since 2003: the crunch of graham, the melt of chocolate, the soft landing of marshmallow. Some cereals you eat. This one you campaign for — and then you eat it, preferably while planning the next petition, just in case.
